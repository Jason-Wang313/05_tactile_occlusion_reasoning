\begin{abstract}
Robots often manipulate objects whose decisive geometry is hidden from vision:
the back edge of a handle, the mouth of an insertion channel, the lip of a
transparent cup, or the pocket that makes a grasp stable. Most visuo-tactile
methods treat touch as another route to reconstructing the full object, or as
generic information gain over a dense hidden state. This paper argues for a
different central representation. For a pending manipulation action, hidden
geometries should be grouped into contact-equivalence classes: two geometries
are equivalent when every currently relevant contact would have the same
outcome. Tactile probes should split these classes, not necessarily reconstruct
the entire censored surface. We formalize the representation, map its novelty
against 1332 related works, and provide a deterministic synthetic manipulation
testbed. In the testbed, vision censors a hidden insertion channel and many
irrelevant decorative bits. Dense entropy reduction spends probes on irrelevant
geometry and reaches 10.2\% success at four probes; critical-cell entropy
reaches 41.0\%; contact-equivalence reaches 100.0\% by asking balanced tactile
sweeps over contact outcomes. The result is not a claim of real-robot
deployment. It is a falsification of a common field assumption: that reducing
uncertainty over visually hidden geometry is the same thing as reducing
manipulation-relevant ambiguity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Robotic touch is usually justified by what vision cannot see. A camera cannot
observe the backside of an object during grasping, the interior boundary of a
slot during insertion, or the true surface of transparent and reflective
objects. Modern tactile work has therefore made substantial progress on
sensors, contact geometry, object pose, visuo-tactile fusion, and shape
reconstruction \citep{seminara2019active,tahoun2021visual,bauza2023tac2pose,comi2024touchsdf,huang2024normalflow,lee2025vitascope,gu2026touchanything}. The implicit bargain is:
when vision censors geometry, touch should help recover that geometry.

This bargain is often too strong for manipulation. If the robot needs to decide
whether a planned insertion will collide or pass, then many hidden shapes are
irrelevant. They differ as dense geometry, but not as contact outcomes for the
next action. Conversely, two visually plausible shapes may look equally likely
under a reconstruction prior while inducing different collision, slip, or stable
pocket outcomes. The robot does not need the whole backside. It needs to know
which contact outcome class it is in.

We propose \emph{contact-equivalence reasoning}: maintain a partition over
hidden geometries induced by the manipulation contacts that matter now, and
choose tactile probes that split this partition. This changes the central
mechanism from shape recovery to task-indexed equivalence. It is a small
representational shift, but it changes which touches are valuable. A probe that
reduces dense surface entropy can be useless for the current action, while a
probe that leaves most geometry unknown can eliminate all decision ambiguity.

The contribution is deliberately narrow:

\begin{itemize}
    \item We define contact-equivalence classes for visually censored geometry
    and state the decision condition under which unresolved geometric ambiguity
    is harmless.
    \item We pressure-test the novelty boundary against a 1332-paper landscape
    sweep, including 300 serious skim papers, 240 deep-read rows, and 100
    hostile prior works saved with the repository.
    \item We implement a deterministic synthetic manipulation environment that
    isolates the failure mode. Contact-equivalence succeeds with four probes,
    while dense entropy reduction keeps spending probes on irrelevant hidden
    bits.
\end{itemize}

The paper does not introduce a new tactile sensor, a larger model, a benchmark
as the main contribution, a learned foundation policy, or a real-robot system.
Its purpose is to make one assumption visible and runnable: hidden geometry
should not be represented at a granularity finer than the manipulation contacts
that can distinguish it.

\section{Related Work and Novelty Boundary}

\paragraph{Tactile sensors and contact geometry.}
Vision-based and force-based tactile sensors have improved the fidelity with
which robots observe local contact. GelStereo-style calibration
\citep{zhang2023gelstereo}, marker-based dense tactile reconstruction
\citep{xue2023dense}, omnidirectional tactile cameras \citep{tippur2023gelsight360},
large-area skins \citep{hu2024magnetic}, and deformable tactile arrays
\citep{jiang2024stretchable} increase spatial coverage and measurement quality.
These works make contact observable. They do not, by themselves, say which
unknown parts of an object should remain unresolved because they are equivalent
for the pending manipulation.

\paragraph{Shape reconstruction from touch.}
Touch has been used to infer object shape from sparse contacts, often with
implicit or generative models \citep{tahoun2021visual,comi2024touchsdf,zhang2025diffusion,gu2026touchanything}. Active tactile reconstruction asks where to touch
next to recover more of the object \citep{ottenhaus2018active,oikonomou2025proactive}.
This literature directly threatens novelty if our claim is merely ``touch helps
recover hidden shape.'' We therefore do not make that claim. The proposed
mechanism can stop before full reconstruction: if all remaining geometries
produce the same relevant contact outcome, they should be collapsed for the
current decision.

\paragraph{Pose, localization, and visuo-tactile fusion.}
Tactile observations are also used for pose estimation, tracking, and in-hand
state inference \citep{bauza2023tac2pose,kissoum2021simultaneous,huang2024normalflow,lee2025vitascope}. Other systems fuse vision and touch for contact-rich
policies or multimodal representation learning \citep{du2022play,zhan2026forceflow}.
These systems often assume that the latent object state is shared across
modalities. Our setting instead treats vision as a censoring process that maps
many contact-different geometries to the same visual observation. The problem is
not only fusing two feature streams; it is representing the equivalence class
left by a censored visual channel.

\paragraph{Active haptic perception.}
Active touch has a long history in robotics \citep{seminara2019active}. The
closest family optimizes information gain or task-aware exploration
\citep{ottenhaus2018active,jiang2022active}. Our difference is not that the
robot acts to gather information. The difference is the random variable whose
information matters. Dense object entropy, contact-cell entropy, and
contact-outcome entropy can rank the same probe library very differently. The
experiment in Section~\ref{sec:evidence} is designed to make that separation
unavoidable.

\section{Contact-Equivalence Reasoning}

Let $g \in \Geom$ denote the hidden geometry of an object and $o = V(g)$ the
visual observation after a censoring map $V$. The posterior after vision is
$p(g \mid o)$. A manipulation action $a \in \Act$ interacts with geometry only
through a contact-outcome map
\begin{equation}
    C_a : \Geom \rightarrow \Outcome,
\end{equation}
where $\Outcome$ may encode collision, free passage, slip, stable pocket,
or another action-relevant contact event.

\paragraph{Definition.}
For a set of currently relevant actions $A \subseteq \Act$, two hidden
geometries $g_i,g_j$ are contact-equivalent when
\begin{equation}
    g_i \sim_A g_j \quad \Longleftrightarrow \quad
    C_a(g_i)=C_a(g_j) \;\; \text{for every } a \in A.
\end{equation}
The quotient $\Geom / {\sim_A}$ is the contact-equivalence partition. A tactile
probe $t \in \Touch$ has observation model $p(z \mid g,t)$. Its value is not
measured by how much it reduces entropy over $\Geom$, but by how much it reduces
uncertainty over the quotient cells that change the decision.

\paragraph{Decision condition.}
Suppose the task loss for selecting action $a$ depends on geometry only through
the contact outcome: $\loss(a,g)=\bar{\loss}(a,C_a(g))$. If all posterior mass
after observations lies in one contact-equivalence class for the chosen action
set $A$, then any two geometries in the support induce identical losses for
actions in $A$. In that case, further reconstruction inside the class cannot
improve the Bayes decision over $A$. This is a modest formal claim, but it is
the key representational lever: uncertainty can remain high over dense geometry
while decision ambiguity is zero.

\paragraph{Probe objective.}
For a belief $b(g)$ and probe $t$, the dense reconstruction objective is
\begin{equation}
    \Delta H_\mathrm{dense}(t)=H_b(G)-\mathbb{E}_{z}[H_{b(\cdot\mid z,t)}(G)].
\end{equation}
Contact-equivalence instead optimizes entropy over quotient cells $Q_A(G)$:
\begin{equation}
    \Delta H_\mathrm{contact}(t)=
    H_b(Q_A(G))-\mathbb{E}_{z}[H_{b(\cdot\mid z,t)}(Q_A(G))].
\end{equation}
The two objectives coincide only when all dense geometric degrees of freedom are
decision-relevant. Under visual occlusion that is rarely true: decorative,
backside, or off-path geometry may dominate dense entropy while having no effect
on the action being planned.

\section{Controlled Evidence}
\label{sec:evidence}

We implement a minimal hidden-geometry manipulation world in
\texttt{experiments/tactile\_occlusion\_sim.py}. Vision observes that an object
contains one hidden insertion channel but censors which of 12 lanes is open. It
also censors 64 decorative bits. The robot succeeds only if it selects the true
hidden channel. Every method receives the same probe budget and the same probe
library. The methods differ only in objective:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Visual only} selects from the visual posterior without touch.
    \item \textbf{Dense entropy} greedily reduces entropy of the full hidden
    state, including decorative bits.
    \item \textbf{Critical-cell entropy} probes single lane cells and reduces
    uncertainty over the contact variable one lane at a time.
    \item \textbf{Contact-equivalence} chooses tactile sweeps that split the
    posterior over lane-outcome classes.
\end{itemize}

The environment is intentionally stylized. Its value is not realism; its value
is that the hidden assumption is controlled. If generic hidden-geometry
uncertainty were the right objective, dense entropy should not fail when given
the same probe budget. If the equivalence partition matters, dense entropy can
be provably distracted by irrelevant hidden variables.

Table~\ref{tab:main} shows the main result over 2000 trials per setting with
seed 505. With four probes, contact-equivalence reaches 100.0\% success.
Critical-cell entropy reaches 41.0\% because it asks lane-is-$i$ questions one
at a time. Dense entropy reaches only 10.2\%, near the visual-only baseline,
because its first probes are consumed by high-entropy decorative variables that
do not affect the contact decision.

Table~\ref{tab:noise} adds binary observation noise to the tactile probes. At

at 10\%, 68.3\%; and at 20\%, 45.2\%. This supports only a limited robustness
claim. It does not establish performance with real tactile sensors, deformable
objects, or high-dimensional 3D geometry.

\section{What The Experiment Proves and Does Not Prove}

The experiment demonstrates three statements. First, the same visual observation
can correspond to many hidden geometries with different manipulation outcomes.
Second, dense reconstruction uncertainty can be dominated by variables that are
irrelevant to the current action. Third, a tactile action chosen to split
contact-equivalence classes can require fewer probes than a dense
reconstruction objective.

The experiment does not prove that exact quotient maintenance is tractable in
full 3D. It does not show that real tactile observations are clean enough to
identify contact outcomes without calibration drift. It does not compare against
large learned visuo-tactile policies on hardware. Those are real weaknesses.
They are also the reason the claim is framed as a mechanism and representation
claim rather than a deployment claim.

\section{Reproducibility and Literature Audit}

The repository contains all generated artifacts. The literature pipeline
queries OpenAlex for tactile perception, visuo-tactile manipulation, hidden
geometry, active haptics, contact sensing, and robot manipulation terms. It
produced 1332 rows in \texttt{docs/related\_work\_matrix.csv}. The documents
\texttt{docs/literature\_map.md}, \texttt{docs/hostile\_prior\_work.md},
\texttt{docs/novelty\_boundary\_map.md}, and
\texttt{docs/novelty\_decision.md} record the landscape sweep, the 300-paper
serious skim, the 240-paper deep-read extraction protocol, the 100-paper
hostile set, at least 20 hidden assumptions, rejected paper directions, and the
chosen thesis.

The experiment can be reproduced with:
\begin{verbatim}
python experiments/tactile_occlusion_sim.py
\end{verbatim}
When the cached outputs are complete, the script exits quickly and reuses them.
Use \texttt{--force} to regenerate the full 2000-trial sweeps.

\section{Conclusion}

Tactile occlusion reasoning should not automatically mean reconstructing the
occluded object. For manipulation, the useful latent variable is often a
partition over contact outcomes. Contact-equivalence classes make this explicit:
they preserve distinctions that change the action and collapse distinctions
that do not. The controlled evidence shows a concrete failure mode for dense
uncertainty reduction under visual censoring and a simple route around it. The
next step is to approximate these partitions in real 3D manipulation systems,
where the central challenge will be learning or computing contact-outcome maps
without losing the decision-level discipline that makes the representation
useful.
